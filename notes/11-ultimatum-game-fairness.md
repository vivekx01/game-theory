# 11 — The Ultimatum Game & Fairness

## The setup

There's $10 to split. The **Proposer** offers a split (e.g. "I keep $9,
you get $1"). The **Responder** can only **Accept** (split happens as
proposed) or **Reject** (both people get **$0**). One shot, no back and
forth.

## What "rational" theory predicts

Same backward-induction logic as [[05-sequential-games-credible-threats]]
— look at the Responder's actual payoffs at the moment of decision: given
any positive offer, the choice is between that amount and $0. Something
beats nothing, so standard theory predicts the Responder should accept
*any* positive offer, however small. Knowing this, the Proposer should
offer the smallest amount possible and keep nearly everything.

## What real people actually do

Across many real experiments, in many countries, a large share of people
— often close to half — **reject** very lopsided offers (around 90/10 or
worse), even knowing it means walking away with literally nothing instead
of something.

This isn't irrationality so much as a different value being weighed:
people have a real aversion to being treated unfairly, strong enough to
pay a real cost just to "punish" an insultingly low offer.

### Why this is actually a sound strategy, not just emotion

A willingness to reject unfair splits — even once — functions as a
credible signal that you won't be exploited, exactly like
**Grim Trigger** from [[04-repeated-games-shadow-of-the-future]]. If
you're known to reject lowball offers, people learn not to lowball you.
The amount "lost" in the moment is effectively buying a reputation that
pays off in every future negotiation.

## Where this matters in real life

**If you're proposing a split** (a salary offer, a rent split, a
contract term): don't anchor to the theoretical minimum and expect
acceptance just because it's technically better than nothing. Perceived
fairness is a hard constraint on whether a deal closes at all, not a
nice-to-have — a technically-rational-but-insulting offer can blow up a
deal a fair one would've closed easily.

**If you're receiving an offer:** being willing to walk away from a
genuinely unfair one, even at short-term cost, is a legitimate long-term
strategy, not just a feeling — connects to
[[09-bargaining-theory-batna]] and credible threats. People and
organizations remember who folds and who doesn't, and that memory shapes
every future offer you get.

**The bigger takeaway:** pure payoff-maximization doesn't fully predict
real human behavior — fairness is a real force in negotiations, not a
soft add-on, and ignoring it is itself a strategic mistake.
