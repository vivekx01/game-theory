# 06 — Asymmetric Information & Signaling

## The setup

So far every game assumed both players know all the payoffs and rules.
Real life often isn't like that — one side knows something important the
other doesn't (true quality of a product, true skill of a candidate, true
intentions of a negotiator).

## The Market for Lemons

Used cars: half are **Good** (worth $20,000 to a buyer, seller values at
$15,000), half are **Lemons** (worth $8,000 to a buyer, seller values at
$5,000). The seller always knows which type their car is; the buyer can't
tell just by looking.

### The spiral (adverse selection)

1. Buyer can't tell type apart → offers the average: (20,000 + 8,000) / 2
   = **$14,000**.
2. At $14,000: Lemon owners happily sell (way above their $5,000 floor).
   Good owners walk away (it's below their $15,000 floor — selling would be
   a loss).
3. Buyer anticipates this and realizes: *only Lemons will actually show up
   at that price.* So they revise the offer down to roughly what a Lemon
   is worth — **~$8,000**.
4. At $8,000, still only Lemons sell. Good cars **never sell**, even though
   a Good-car buyer and seller both would have happily traded somewhere
   between $15,000–$20,000.

**The punchline:** pure information asymmetry destroyed a mutually
beneficial trade, with nobody lying or acting irrationally — just because
the buyer couldn't verify quality. Bad quality drove out good. This spiral
is called **adverse selection**, and the end state — where both types look
identical and only the worst type actually trades — is a **pooling
equilibrium**.

## The fix: costly signaling

Just *telling* the buyer "trust me, it's a great car" doesn't work — a
Lemon owner can say the exact same words for free. Worthless claims like
this are "cheap talk," and rational players ignore them.

A signal only works if it's **costly, and differently costly for each
type** — cheap for the genuinely good type, expensive for the bad type.

**Warranty example:** a Good car owner can offer a warranty cheaply (the
car rarely breaks, so it rarely gets used). A Lemon owner offering the same
warranty would get crushed by real repair costs. Because a Lemon owner
can't profitably copy the move, a buyer seeing a warranty can actually
trust it — not based on the seller's word, but based on the economics
behind the signal.

When a costly signal splits the good and bad types into different,
observable behaviors (e.g. some sellers offer warranties, some don't
bother), that's called a **separating equilibrium** — the opposite of the
pooling trap above.

## Why education signals ability even when course content is irrelevant

Who finds it genuinely cheaper (less time, less struggle, lower risk of
failing out) to get through a hard degree — a capable, disciplined person,
or someone who isn't? The first type finds it annoying but doable; the
second finds it genuinely hard. So simply **surviving** the degree reveals
something real about the holder, regardless of whether the class content
itself matters for the job. This only works *because* the degree is hard —
if it were free/easy for everyone, it would signal nothing (just like
cheap talk), and employers would stop caring about it.

## Where this shows up in real life

- Warranties and guarantees on products
- College degrees, professional certifications
- Expensive engagement rings — a weak/uncommitted partner wouldn't bother
- Startup founders investing their own savings — signals confidence a
  scammer wouldn't risk
- Heavy, sustained brand advertising — only a company expecting long-term
  survival benefits from it, so it signals staying power
- Unpaid internships / trial periods — costly to a candidate who isn't
  actually serious or capable
- Peacock tails in biology (classic "costly signal" example outside
  economics) — a weak peacock couldn't afford such an expensive, exposed
  display and survive

**How to use this:**
- As a buyer/evaluator: don't trust cheap claims — look for signals that
  would genuinely cost the other side more if they were lying (warranties,
  skin-in-the-game, track records, things that are expensive to fake).
- As the "Good" party trying to prove it: find or create a signal that's
  cheap for you specifically but would be expensive for a worse version of
  you to copy — that's what actually earns trust, not just stating your
  case.
