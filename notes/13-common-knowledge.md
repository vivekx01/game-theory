# 13 — Common Knowledge vs. Private Knowledge

## The puzzle

A meeting where everyone privately thinks the plan is bad, nobody says
anything, then one person finally speaks up — and immediately several
others agree, and the whole room shifts. Most of those people already
secretly believed it. So what actually changed the moment someone said
it out loud?

## Why silence can be stable even when almost everyone agrees

From any one person's view before anyone speaks: you personally doubt
the plan, but you don't know if anyone else does. For all you know,
you're the only skeptic, and speaking up alone makes you look foolish. So
you stay quiet. If everyone in the room reasons the same way, the room
can sit in total silence indefinitely — not because the doubt is rare,
but because it's **invisible to itself**. Numerically, almost everyone
can agree, and the group can still never act on it.

## Private knowledge vs. common knowledge

There's a real distinction between:
- **Everyone individually knowing something** (private/mutual knowledge)
- **Common knowledge**: I know it, I know you know it, you know I know
  it, and so on, without end

Telling people individually and privately ("between us, I think this
plan is bad") only creates the first kind. It does nothing to unlock the
second, because each person still has no idea the others got the same
message.

Only a **public, simultaneous** event — someone saying it out loud in the
room, where everyone sees everyone else hear it — converts private doubt
into common knowledge. That's the actual moment the room shifts: not
because new information arrived, but because everyone now knows that
everyone else knows too, which makes it finally safe to act on what they
already believed.

## Bank runs: the same mechanism at scale

Depositors privately suspect a bank is shaky but stay calm individually,
each hoping it's just their own paranoia — until a public signal (a news
report, a visible line forming outside a branch) makes the doubt common
knowledge all at once. The run happens almost instantly, even though the
bank's actual finances didn't change in that exact moment. The information
was already there; what changed was who-knows-that-who-knows.

## Connects back to the Volunteer's Dilemma

This deepens [[10-volunteers-dilemma-bystander-effect]]: it's not just
"someone else will probably do it" holding people back from speaking up
— it's that nobody even knows whether speaking up would get backed by
anyone else in the room. Both effects stack.

## Where this shows up in real life

- Team meetings where a bad decision sails through despite widespread
  private doubt
- Social/political movements that look like they erupt "suddenly" — the
  underlying sentiment was often already widespread, just never public
- Bank runs and market panics
- Workplace culture problems that persist for years until one public
  complaint (a group email, a public post) breaks the dam

**How to use this:** if you believe something unpopular is actually
widely held privately, the bottleneck usually isn't convincing more
people — it's making the belief **visible to everyone at once**. Say it
in the actual meeting, not just to people one-on-one afterward. Write the
group email instead of several private messages. The goal isn't new
information; it's making sure everyone knows that everyone else knows.
