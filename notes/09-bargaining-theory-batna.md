# 09 — Bargaining Theory & BATNA

## The setup

Two parties can create value together (a lease, a job, a partnership), but
only by agreeing on how to split it. If they can't agree, both walk away
with nothing from the deal (or with their individual fallback).

Example: a landlord and tenant could jointly create $100 of combined
value (tenant values the place $100 more than their next best option,
combined with landlord's costs) — but they disagree on how that $100
splits between lower rent and higher landlord profit.

## What actually determines the split

The counterintuitive result from formal bargaining theory (the **Nash
Bargaining Solution**): if both sides have equally good walk-away
alternatives and are equally willing to walk away, the surplus splits
**50/50** — regardless of who argues better or more persuasively.

The split only moves away from 50/50 when one side's **BATNA — Best
Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement** (what you get if this deal falls
through and you walk away) — is genuinely stronger than the other's, or
one side can afford more patience (less urgency to close).

### Why "arguing better" isn't really the lever

A tenant with a solid backup apartment lined up gets a better rent deal —
even if they never use that backup — purely because the landlord knows
the tenant can walk away cheaply. Same with a job offer in hand during
salary negotiation: it shifts the outcome dramatically, even if the actual
conversation and your delivery are identical either way.

Good negotiators usually aren't winning through clever rhetoric — they're
winning because they built or revealed a strong alternative *before* the
conversation. The negotiation itself is mostly where existing leverage
gets revealed, not where it gets created.

### Connects back to credible threats

"I'll walk away" is only real leverage if it's a credible threat, same as
[[05-sequential-games-credible-threats]] — it only works if your BATNA is
actually real, not bluffed. An empty "I have other options" claim is just
cheap talk, same idea as [[06-asymmetric-information-signaling]].

## Where this shows up in real life

- Salary negotiation — having another offer (even one you wouldn't take)
  moves the outcome more than any phrasing or tactic in the room
- Rent/lease negotiation — a genuine backup apartment changes your terms
- Business deals — the side that can walk away most comfortably usually
  gets the better split, independent of who negotiates more skillfully
- Any "take it or leave it" dynamic — whoever needs the deal less, wins
  more of it

**How to use this:** before any negotiation, the highest-leverage move
isn't rehearsing arguments — it's strengthening your actual walk-away
option beforehand (apply elsewhere first, get a second buyer interested,
line up a real backup). That single move affects the outcome more than
anything said in the actual conversation.
