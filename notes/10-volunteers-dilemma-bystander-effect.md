# 10 — Volunteer's Dilemma & the Bystander Effect

## The setup

A group all benefits if just **one** person takes on a costly task
(reporting a problem, fixing something shared, speaking up) — but nobody
wants to be the one who pays that cost if they can get away with someone
else doing it instead.

Two-person version:

|                     | Other reports | Other stays quiet |
|---------------------|----------------|---------------------|
| **You report**      | 8, 8           | 8, 10               |
| **You stay quiet**  | 10, 8          | 0, 0                |

(Reporting costs a bit of effort — net 8 instead of 10 — but if nobody
reports, the problem festers and everyone gets 0.)

### The best-response logic

If you're confident the other person won't act, you should — someone has
to, or you both get 0. If you're confident they will act, you shouldn't
bother — free-ride and take the full 10. Same anti-coordination shape as
[[08-evolutionary-game-theory-hawk-dove]]: nobody wants to be the one who
"concedes" by volunteering, but somebody has to, or everyone loses.

## Scaling it up: the bystander effect

Now scale the same logic to a large group — say, 20 witnesses instead of
2. You'd intuitively expect more witnesses to make it *more* likely
someone acts. The opposite tends to happen, and this is the real
explanation behind the famous "bystander effect": each individual
reasons, often unconsciously, "surely with this many people around,
someone else will handle it." Each person's own likelihood of stepping up
actually **drops** as the group grows — responsibility gets diffused
across everyone and ends up concentrated on no one.

This isn't about people being callous or badly behaved. It's the same
kind of structural, automatic outcome as the Hawk-Dove ratio settling on
its own — nobody needs to consciously calculate anything for this pattern
to emerge. The real culprit is the **ambiguity** of "someone should do
this."

## The fix: collapse the ambiguity

The well-documented, actually-works fix for an emergency: point at one
specific person and say **"you — call 911,"** instead of shouting
"somebody call for help" to a crowd. This converts a diffuse group
dilemma (where waiting might be each person's best move) into a direct,
unambiguous individual decision — now ignoring it is obviously on that
one person.

## Where this shows up in real life

- Group chats / team channels — posting "can someone look into this?" to
  a group predictably produces less action than naming one person
  directly, even though the group is "more capable" collectively
- Group projects — vague shared ownership of a task reliably produces
  under-delivery; naming a specific owner fixes it
- Household chores among roommates — "someone should clean this" rarely
  works; "can you clean this by Friday" does
- Emergencies and public safety — the "point at someone specific" advice
  is literally taught in first-aid/CPR training for this exact reason

**How to use this:** whenever you need something done in a group and it
isn't happening, the fix usually isn't "ask again" or "ask more
forcefully" — it's removing the ambiguity about *whose* job it is.
Assign a specific name to a specific task, every time. The dilemma
disappears once there's no one left to diffuse responsibility onto.
