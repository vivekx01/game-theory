# Decision Framework — "What Game Am I Actually In?"

A step-by-step protocol to run through on any tense, high-stakes, or
conflicted situation — a negotiation, an argument, a business decision,
someone making you a promise or threat. The goal isn't to be a genius in
the moment; it's to have the questions already decided in advance, so
there's nothing to invent under pressure.

## Step 0 — The one thing to actually remember in the moment

**Strong reaction = pause before responding.**

That's the only thing that needs to fire automatically. Everything below
can happen in the 10-60 seconds after the pause, out loud or in your head
— it does not need to be instant.

## Step 1 — Is this one-time or repeated?

> Will I deal with this person / this situation again?

- **One-time** → the "shadow of the future" is weak. Don't expect
  long-term reciprocity logic to protect you here — guard your downside.
  But also: low long-term cost if it goes badly.
- **Repeated / ongoing** → protecting the relationship usually matters more
  than winning this one round. Reputation and future dealings are doing
  most of the work here, not this single moment.
  → see [[04-repeated-games-shadow-of-the-future]]

## Step 2 — Does my best move depend on what they do?

> If I imagine them doing A vs. B, does my own best response actually change?

- **No, same best move either way** → this smells like a Prisoner's
  Dilemma — possibly a trust/incentive trap, not a question of who's
  smarter or more moral. Ask: can the *incentives* be fixed (contract,
  transparency, smaller trial commitment) instead of just hoping people
  act better?
  → see [[01-intro-prisoners-dilemma]]

  **But first check which disease this actually is:** is anyone genuinely
  tempted to defect for personal gain (greed — a real Prisoner's
  Dilemma), or would everyone clearly prefer the cooperative outcome and
  the only issue is not being sure the other will show up (fear/trust —
  a Stag Hunt)? These need opposite fixes: incentives/enforcement for
  greed, visible commitment signals for fear. Treating a trust problem
  like a greed problem (more rules, more punishment) won't fix it.
  → see [[12-stag-hunt-trust-vs-greed]]
- **Yes, it depends on them** → go to Step 3.

## Step 3 — Do we actually want the same thing?

> Strip away the specific disagreement — do we both want to cooperate /
> reach a good outcome, just via different paths?

- **Yes** → this is a coordination problem, not a logic problem (Battle of
  the Sexes). Stop arguing about who's "right" — pick a convention, default,
  or precedent and move on. Arguing harder won't fix a coordination problem.
  → see [[02-nash-equilibrium-battle-of-sexes]]
- **No, it's genuinely adversarial / zero-sum** → avoid settling into a
  predictable pattern; deliberately vary your approach.
  → see [[03-mixed-strategies-matching-pennies]]

## Step 4 — Is there a threat or promise on the table?

> Would they actually prefer to follow through, once the moment of truth
> arrives — looking at *their* incentives then, not what they're saying now?

- **Not credible (empty threat/promise)** → don't let it move you.
- **If it's your own threat/promise** → words alone won't be believed.
  Make backing out genuinely costly for yourself first (public commitment,
  a real penalty, skin in the game).
  → see [[05-sequential-games-credible-threats]]

## Step 5 — Is trust or betrayal the actual issue?

> Is this clear, provable betrayal — or could it plausibly be a mistake,
> miscommunication, or noise?

- **Clear, provable** → cut it off for good (Grim Trigger). Forgiving a
  proven betrayal just invites it again.
- **Ambiguous / could be a mistake** → respond, but stay open to repair
  (Tit-for-Tat / Tit-for-Two-Tats). Don't torch a good relationship over
  something you're not actually sure happened on purpose.
  → see [[04-repeated-games-shadow-of-the-future]]

## Step 6 — Am I just being asked to take someone's word for it?

> Is there an actual costly signal here, or just a claim?

- **Just a claim / cheap talk** → don't trust it by default.
- **Costly signal** → ask: would this cost a *fake* version of them more
  to produce than it costs the real deal? If yes, it's worth trusting more.
  → see [[06-asymmetric-information-signaling]]

---

### How to actually use this doc

Don't try to memorize all six steps. Bookmark/reread this file itself in
the moment if you can (even mid-conversation, excuse yourself for a
minute) — the point of writing it down is so you don't have to hold it in
your head. Over time, running through it repeatedly is what eventually
makes it automatic — same as [[04-repeated-games-shadow-of-the-future]]
explained about building intuition through repeated deliberate practice.
